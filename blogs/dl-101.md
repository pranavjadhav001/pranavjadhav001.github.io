      <h2 id="what-is-ml-vs-dl">What is ML, and how is it different from DL?</h2>
      <p>How I see and interpret it is: when you have data from an observed event, and you look at that data, you've learned some information &mdash; in better words, a prior, or weights. Based on this information you've attained, you can now make an educated prediction:</p>
      <ul>
        <li>Like logging weather information &mdash; predicting whether it will rain based on how many years you've been observing</li>
        <li>Predicting rent prices in Bangalore</li>
        <li>How much time you'll take to reach office on Thursday, given how you've observed and logged your Thursdays</li>
      </ul>
      <p>So in layman's terms, ML is nothing but pattern recognition / matching &mdash; data-driven mental models, assumptions, and rules of a closed, observable system.</p>

      <h3>How to see it in software terms</h3>
      <p>When you're developing a feature, you get requirements and some initial assumptions from stakeholders. Based on these assumptions you build a model and some sample data, and handle your scenarios with if-elses and complex branching. Later you test in a QA environment, which surfaces broader scenarios your code (or model) wasn't ready for, so it breaks. You understand these new scenarios and bake in the assumptions. Later you deploy to production, where even broader scenarios show up and your model starts to fail again. You bake those in too, and your accuracy &mdash; or reliability &mdash; improves.</p>
      <p>Just like your software improved with data, so do ML models. The real difference is in the rules: in software, you hand-craft the rules; in ML, you learn the rules.</p>
      <p><em>(this analogy I learned from Karpathy's "Software 2.0" blog)</em></p>

      <h3>How were these weights learned?</h3>
      <p>The model doesn't guess <code>m</code> and <code>c</code> out of thin air &mdash; it learns them by repeatedly checking how wrong it is, and using that error to nudge itself in the right direction. That "how wrong" number, for a single data point, is called a <b>residual</b>.</p>

      <pre class="formula">residual = actual_rent − predicted_rent
         = actual_rent − (m × sqft + c)</pre>

      <p>Watch it happen: start from a flatly wrong guess (<code>m = 0</code>, <code>c = 0</code>, predicting every rental costs nothing), and nudge <code>m</code> and <code>c</code> a few times using exactly that residual math.</p>

      <p>Written out, this is the update every epoch performs. Here's where <code>grad_m</code> and <code>grad_c</code> actually come from, differentiating the loss (per point <code>i</code>, chain rule throughout):</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$\text{residual}_i = \text{actual}_i - (m \cdot \text{sqft}_i + c) \qquad L = \sum_i \text{residual}_i^2$$
      $$\begin{aligned}
      \frac{\partial L}{\partial m} &= \sum_i 2\,\text{residual}_i \cdot \frac{\partial\,\text{residual}_i}{\partial m} \\
      &= \sum_i 2\,\text{residual}_i \cdot (-\text{sqft}_i) \\
      &= -2\sum_i \left(\text{residual}_i \times \text{sqft}_i\right)
      \end{aligned}$$
      $$\begin{aligned}
      \frac{\partial L}{\partial c} &= \sum_i 2\,\text{residual}_i \cdot \frac{\partial\,\text{residual}_i}{\partial c} \\
      &= \sum_i 2\,\text{residual}_i \cdot (-1) \\
      &= -2\sum_i \text{residual}_i
      \end{aligned}$$
      $$\text{grad}_m = \frac{\partial L}{\partial m} \propto -\sum(\text{residual} \times \text{sqft}) \qquad \text{grad}_c = \frac{\partial L}{\partial c} \propto -\sum(\text{residual})$$
      </div>
      <p>(The constant 2 above is dropped in <code>grad_m</code>/<code>grad_c</code>, it just folds into the learning rate.)</p>

      <pre class="formula">m, c = 0, 0                               # start with a guess

for epoch in range(N):
    predicted = m * sqft + c              # predict
    residual  = actual_rent - predicted   # how wrong, per rental
    loss      = sum(residual ** 2)        # the number we're shrinking

    grad_m = -sum(residual * sqft)        # &prop; direction to nudge m
    grad_c = -sum(residual)               # &prop; direction to nudge c

    m -= learning_rate * grad_m           # nudge the weights
    c -= learning_rate * grad_c</pre>

      <h2 id="lets-talk-about-gradient-descent">Let's talk about gradient descent</h2>
      <p>You choose a loss that defines the goodness of fit, the objective of whatever you're learning, and then you minimize it: the gap between what your model predicts and the actual ground truth. How do you do that? <strong>Using gradient descent:</strong> you nudge the weights of your parameters in the direction that gets you closer to the minimum loss.</p>

      <p><strong>What is a gradient?</strong> It's the slope. It tells you which direction, and how much, you will the function move at a given point.</p>

      <p>Take <code>f(x) = x&sup2;</code>, with derivative <code>f'(x) = 2x</code>, which tells you the slope at any point <code>x</code>:</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$f(x) = x^2 \qquad f'(x) = 2x$$
      </div>

      <p>If you're at <code>x = 3</code>, the slope is <code>6</code>, meaning the function is increasing steeply to the right. If you want to decrease <code>f</code>, you should move to the left, in the opposite direction of the slope.</p>

      <div class="lr-widget">
        <iframe id="slopeFrame" src="/blogs/dl-101/slope_descent_chalk.html" title="Chalkboard figure: a point sliding down f(x) = x squared via gradient descent, opposite the slope's direction" loading="lazy" style="width:100%;height:420px;border:0;display:block"></iframe>

            <text x="560" y="325" class="shapetxt" text-anchor="middle">&part;L/&part;W2</text>
            <text x="420" y="325" class="shapetxt" text-anchor="middle">&part;L/&part;h</text>

          </svg>
        </figure>
        <figcaption>
          <p>Teal is the forward pass, values flowing left to right. Dashed red is the backward pass: the same path, run in reverse, carrying gradients instead of values. Each backward hop multiplies in the local derivative of the operation it crosses, that's the chain rule.</p>
        </figcaption>
      </div>

      <p>Written out, the two gradients that matter are <code>&part;L/&part;W2</code> and <code>&part;L/&part;W1</code>:</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_2} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial W_2} = \underbrace{-2(y-\hat{y})}_{\partial L/\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \underbrace{h}_{\partial \hat{y}/\partial W_2}$$
      </div>

      <div class="formula">
      $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial h} \cdot \frac{\partial h}{\partial z_1} \cdot \frac{\partial z_1}{\partial W_1} = \underbrace{-2(y-\hat{y})}_{\partial L/\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \underbrace{W_2}_{\partial \hat{y}/\partial h} \cdot \underbrace{\mathbb{1}[z_1 > 0]}_{\partial h/\partial z_1} \cdot \underbrace{x}_{\partial z_1/\partial W_1}$$
      </div>

      <p><code>W1</code> is four hops from the loss, so its gradient is a product of four local derivatives instead of two. That <code>&#120783;[z1 &gt; 0]</code> term is ReLU's derivative: 1 where the hidden unit fired, 0 where it didn't.</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$W_1 \leftarrow W_1 - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_1} \qquad W_2 \leftarrow W_2 - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_2}$$
      </div>

      <p>Do that thousands of times, over every row, and this is exactly the loop from the rent MLP animation earlier: predict, chain rule back through both layers, nudge every weight its gradient points at, repeat.</p>
    </section>

    <section>
      <h2 id="stochastic-gradient-descent">SGD: why batching is required</h2>
      <p>Now this weight update step, when should we do it, should we run it for the entire dataset and determine gradients then and then do 1 gradient step. Looks SLOW.</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$\nabla L(w) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \nabla L_i(w)$$
      </div>

      <p>Instead what we say is, say we choose a random batch of n samples from our dataset, and run it across our network and compute gradients and then update the weights. Yes the gradient will be noisy, but if the batch is sampled properly, the mini-batch gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full-dataset gradient. Here "stochastic" means random.</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$\nabla L(w) \approx \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i \in \text{batch}} \nabla L_i(w) \qquad B \ll N$$
      </div>

      <h2 id="optimizers">Optimizers</h2>
      <p>You saw this formula before, wonder what <code>&eta;</code> was, see how it can directly influence how the gradient step can change the weight value. This is an optimizer.</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$w \leftarrow w - \eta \, \nabla L(w)$$
      </div>

      <p>An <strong>optimizer</strong> is whatever turns a gradient into that step. The gradient only ever tells you a direction, uphill; the optimizer decides how much of it to actually use. In its plainest form, that decision is just one number: the learning rate, <code>&eta;</code>.</p>

      <p>Lets look at how learning rate value of optimizer affects the training. A very low l.r. takes small cautious steps while big l.r takes large steps and may overshoot the loss minima. An ideal choice of l.r. is essential for stable training and reaching a good minima valley.</p>

    <section>
      <h2 id="what-is-needed-for-dl-to-work">So what is needed for DL to work?</h2>

      <p>It is a <strong>universal function approximator</strong> which learns to find the minimum loss for a given data landscape.</p>

      <p><strong>Having data,</strong> quantity wise, in sheer number, and quality wise, data should have good enough variance of the underlying distribution you want to map. A good dataset should be representative of what the model will see when deployed. A dataset is divided into <strong>train/test/val sets</strong>: you train on the train set, while training you test on the test set and save the best model parameters based on this, and then finally you test on the val set. All three sets are mutually exclusive, and shouldn't have any leakage. With the val set, what you want to see,<strong> Is the model capable of handling unseen data?</strong></p>

      <p><strong>Adding more parameters</strong> is making your model complex: adding more layers, and non-linearity in between them. This helps when your data is complex, to learn the complex mapping of <code>x -&gt; y</code>. But this saturates after a point, after that <strong>adding more parameters means learning noise in the data, phenomenon called Overfitting</strong>. When they say a 70B model, a 10B model, this is what they mean.</p>

      <p><strong>Finding the right loss:</strong> the model learns the function, but it still needs a way to gauge the problem before that.<strong> A loss function tells your model how far you are from the objective, which lets gradient descent tweak the parameters to minimize it</strong>. For instance, for a multiclass classification problem, you want a loss which rewards your model for maximizing the probability of choosing the right class and punishes it heavily when it chooses wrong, so cross-entropy loss is ideal for such scenarios:</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$L_{CE} = -\sum_{c=1}^{C} y_c \ln(\hat{y}_c) = -\ln(\hat{y}_{\text{correct class}})$$
      </div>

      <p>Another good example is image denoising problems, where the final model output and targets are RGB channel images. In such cases, MSE is best, which reduces the error at each pixel level:</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$L_{MSE} = \frac{1}{HWC}\sum_{i=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{W}\sum_{c=1}^{C} \left(I_{i,j,c} - \hat{I}_{i,j,c}\right)^2$$
      </div>

      <p>But we can improve this by adding another loss term like SSIM, which measures the quality of the generated image from a human visual perspective:</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$SSIM(x,y) = \frac{(2\mu_x\mu_y + c_1)(2\sigma_{xy} + c_2)}{(\mu_x^2 + \mu_y^2 + c_1)(\sigma_x^2 + \sigma_y^2 + c_2)} \qquad L_{SSIM} = 1 - SSIM(x,y)$$
      </div>

      <figure class="figure">
        <div class="figure-frame">
          <a href="/blogs/dl-101/ssim_vs_mse.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><img src="/blogs/dl-101/ssim_vs_mse.png" alt="Four versions of the same photo: the original, then three degraded versions that all share nearly identical MSE (around 306 to 309), but whose SSIM scores range from 0.987 down to 0.576, tracking how visibly different they actually look."></a>
        </div>
        <figcaption><p class="table-caption">Three images with almost the same MSE look nothing alike, MSE can't tell them apart. SSIM can: it drops as the visible structure actually degrades.</p></figcaption>
      </figure>

      <p><strong>Next is optimizers</strong>, which we've discussed above. They're an integral part of how the training will go, they're responsible for its stability and for finding a good minima.</p>

      <p><strong>Last is evaluation:</strong> what is the final goal, where will you use this model? You test your model while training and post-training on a chosen few evaluation metrics, depending on your use case. For clinical settings, you design your evals in a way that tells you whether a model is actually helpful or not, even if it might look good on paper based on loss curves.</p>
    </section>

      <h4>Pooling</h4>
      <p>Pooling layer involves reducing the scale of the input. It can be done via MaxPooling or Average Pooling, you are essentially condensing the level of information from a higher scale to a lower scale. The method of reduction can be choosing the max value in a window, or the average value of a window.</p>

        <div class="figure-frame">

      <h4>Normalization layers</h4>
      <p>Even though we normalize the input before it goes into the network, like <code>image / 255.0</code>, so that the input is bounded between 0 and 1, what about normalization between the layers, and why do we care about it?</p>

      <p>Layers are dependent on the output of the preceding layers. If the input distribution is so wide and varying, a layer will just spend all its time during training catching up to this distribution change, instead of actually learning useful patterns and complex relationships.</p>

      <p>Hence BatchNorm came into being. It's simple: if you have <code>n</code> features in a <code>(Batch size, n)</code> input, it does 0-mean, 1-std normalization of each feature across the batch.</p>

      <figure class="figure">
        <div class="figure-frame">
          <img src="/img/blogs/paligemma-3b/batchnorm.png" alt="A grid of rows (batch) by columns (feature), with rows labeled cat, dog, zebra, tree, stone. Each feature column gets its own mean and standard deviation, mu 1 through mu 7 and sigma 1 through sigma 7, computed down that column across the whole batch.">
        </div>
        <figcaption><p class="table-caption">One mean and one standard deviation per feature (per column), computed across every example in the batch (down each row), not across features, not per example.</p></figcaption>
      </figure>

        <div class="figure-frame">

        </div>
        <figcaption><p class="table-caption">Normalize to 0 mean, 1 std first, same as before, then immediately undo the fixed part of that with a learned scale <code>&gamma;</code> and shift <code>&beta;</code>.       </figure>

      <p>How does this help? The inputs to a layer are no longer all over the place, shifting batch to batch, epoch to epoch. You anticipate the input to have a familiar distribution, and it will.</p>

      <p>Since we have some trainable parameters in a BatchNorm layer, how does it work at inference? Which values do you choose, since the batch's mean and variance will change every batch, right? During training, BatchNorm also keeps a running average of the mean and variance it sees, batch after batch, alongside the actual gradient-learned <code>&gamma;</code> and <code>&beta;</code>. At inference, it stops computing fresh statistics from whatever batch happens to walk in, and just uses those running averages instead, then applies the same learned <code>&gamma;</code>, <code>&beta;</code>. That's what makes inference work for a single example at a time: there's no "batch" left to compute a mean and variance from, so it reuses the one it already built up over the entirety of training.</p>
