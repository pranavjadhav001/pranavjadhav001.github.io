      <h2 id="what-is-ml-vs-dl">What is ML, and how is it different from DL?</h2>
      <p>How I see and interpret it is: when you have data from an observed event, and you look at that data, you've learned some information &mdash; in better words, a prior, or weights. Based on this information you've attained, you can now make an educated prediction:</p>
      <ul>
        <li>Like logging weather information &mdash; predicting whether it will rain based on how many years you've been observing</li>
        <li>Predicting rent prices in Bangalore</li>
        <li>How much time you'll take to reach office on Thursday, given how you've observed and logged your Thursdays</li>
      </ul>
      <p>So in layman's terms, ML is nothing but pattern recognition / matching &mdash; data-driven mental models, assumptions, and rules of a closed, observable system.</p>

      <h3>How to see it in software terms</h3>
      <p>When you're developing a feature, you get requirements and some initial assumptions from stakeholders. Based on these assumptions you build a model and some sample data, and handle your scenarios with if-elses and complex branching. Later you test in a QA environment, which surfaces broader scenarios your code (or model) wasn't ready for, so it breaks. You understand these new scenarios and bake in the assumptions. Later you deploy to production, where even broader scenarios show up and your model starts to fail again. You bake those in too, and your accuracy &mdash; or reliability &mdash; improves.</p>
      <p>Just like your software improved with data, so do ML models. The real difference is in the rules: in software, you hand-craft the rules; in ML, you learn the rules.</p>
      <p><em>(this analogy I learned from Karpathy's "Software 2.0" blog)</em></p>

      <h3>How were these weights learned?</h3>
      <p>The model doesn't guess <code>m</code> and <code>c</code> out of thin air &mdash; it learns them by repeatedly checking how wrong it is, and using that error to nudge itself in the right direction. That "how wrong" number, for a single data point, is called a <b>residual</b>.</p>

      <pre class="formula">residual = actual_rent − predicted_rent
         = actual_rent − (m × sqft + c)</pre>

      <p>Watch it happen: start from a flatly wrong guess (<code>m = 0</code>, <code>c = 0</code>, predicting every rental costs nothing), and nudge <code>m</code> and <code>c</code> a few times using exactly that residual math.</p>

      <p>Written out, this is the update every epoch performs. Here's where <code>grad_m</code> and <code>grad_c</code> actually come from, differentiating the loss (per point <code>i</code>, chain rule throughout):</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$\text{residual}_i = \text{actual}_i - (m \cdot \text{sqft}_i + c) \qquad L = \sum_i \text{residual}_i^2$$
      $$\begin{aligned}
      \frac{\partial L}{\partial m} &= \sum_i 2\,\text{residual}_i \cdot \frac{\partial\,\text{residual}_i}{\partial m} \\
      &= \sum_i 2\,\text{residual}_i \cdot (-\text{sqft}_i) \\
      &= -2\sum_i \left(\text{residual}_i \times \text{sqft}_i\right)
      \end{aligned}$$
      $$\begin{aligned}
      \frac{\partial L}{\partial c} &= \sum_i 2\,\text{residual}_i \cdot \frac{\partial\,\text{residual}_i}{\partial c} \\
      &= \sum_i 2\,\text{residual}_i \cdot (-1) \\
      &= -2\sum_i \text{residual}_i
      \end{aligned}$$
      $$\text{grad}_m = \frac{\partial L}{\partial m} \propto -\sum(\text{residual} \times \text{sqft}) \qquad \text{grad}_c = \frac{\partial L}{\partial c} \propto -\sum(\text{residual})$$
      </div>
      <p>(The constant 2 above is dropped in <code>grad_m</code>/<code>grad_c</code>, it just folds into the learning rate.)</p>

      <pre class="formula">m, c = 0, 0                               # start with a guess

for epoch in range(N):
    predicted = m * sqft + c              # predict
    residual  = actual_rent - predicted   # how wrong, per rental
    loss      = sum(residual ** 2)        # the number we're shrinking

    grad_m = -sum(residual * sqft)        # &prop; direction to nudge m
    grad_c = -sum(residual)               # &prop; direction to nudge c

    m -= learning_rate * grad_m           # nudge the weights
    c -= learning_rate * grad_c</pre>

      <h2 id="how-is-dl-different-than-ml">How is ML different than DL?</h2>
      <p>DL takes away the hand-crafted feature engineering that's essential in ML: it lets the model's parameters decide which features are important and strike the right balance. Just like here, we provide n features we know about the property (floor and sqft) to predict rent, and feed them to the DL model.</p>

      <p>DL is essentially about stacking multiple layers of parameters, one after the other, with a nonlinearity between them. It's that nonlinearity which lets the model learn complex relationships in the data that a straight line can't.</p>

      <p><strong>Could we have done this with plain linear regression instead?</strong> Sure, why not:<br>
      <code>rent &asymp; m1 &times; sqft + m2 &times; floor + c</code>. But that's still linear, a plane instead of a line, same limitation: no matter how you weight sqft and floor, the output is one straight combination of the two, still just one slope. To let the model actually bend, route both features through a small hidden layer first, with a kink in between.</p>

      <p>Substitute <code>h</code> straight in and it's one expression:</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$\hat{y} = W_2 \cdot \text{ReLU}(W_1 x + b_1) + b_2$$
      </div>

      <p><strong>Why is a ReLU, or some nonlinearity, required?</strong> Strip the ReLU out of that and it collapses: <code>ŷ = W2(W1x + b1) + b2 = (W2W1)x + (W2b1 + b2)</code>, one linear layer again, same as the "composing linear functions gives you a bigger linear function" claim from earlier.</p>

      <p>Same nudge-loop as before (predict, residual, loss, gradient, update), just with more numbers to nudge: 6 weights and 3 biases in the first layer (<code>W1</code>, shape 3&times;2), 3 weights and 1 bias in the second (<code>W2</code>, shape 1&times;3), instead of just <code>m</code> and <code>c</code>. Watch what a kink buys you:</p>

      <p>Notice it took thousands of epochs this time, not 3, more numbers to nudge means a slower, bumpier walk down to a good fit. That's not a coincidence: linear regression was only ever searching for the best values of 2 numbers, <code>m</code> and <code>c</code>; this network is searching for the best values of 13 (<code>W1</code>, <code>b1</code>, <code>W2</code>, <code>b2</code>), and the more parameters hunting for their optimum by nudging against the loss, the longer and bumpier that walk gets. That's the actual difference between ML and DL: DL isn't a different kind of learning, it's the same residual-driven nudge-loop, just run on stacked linear layers with a nonlinearity between them. Depth alone buys nothing, composing linear functions just gives you a bigger linear function, so a stack of plain linear layers is still, mathematically, one straight line in disguise. The nonlinearity is what lets it bend, and a real network stacks thousands of these kinks across many layers, more than enough to trace almost any curve you hand it.</p>

      <p>The payoff for the extra time: the model isn't forced to learn one specific function, a line, the way linear regression is. It learns its own function, shaped by whatever the data actually needs.</p>

      <h2 id="lets-talk-about-gradient-descent">Let's talk about gradient descent</h2>
      <p>You choose a loss that defines the goodness of fit, the objective of whatever you're learning, and then you minimize it: the gap between what your model predicts and the actual ground truth. How do you do that? Using gradient descent: you nudge the weights of your parameters in the direction that gets you closer to the minimum loss.</p>

      <p><strong>What is a gradient?</strong> It's the slope. It tells you which direction, and how much, you should move a weight to get closer to your objective.</p>

      <p>Take <code>f(x) = x&sup2;</code>, with derivative <code>f'(x) = 2x</code>, which tells you the slope at any point <code>x</code>:</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$f(x) = x^2 \qquad f'(x) = 2x$$
      </div>

      <p>If you're at <code>x = 3</code>, the slope is <code>6</code>, meaning the function is increasing steeply to the right. If you want to decrease <code>f</code>, you should move to the left, in the opposite direction of the slope.</p>

      <div class="lr-widget">

        <p class="lr-caption">Starting at x = 3, each step moves opposite the slope: x &larr; x &minus; learning_rate &times; f'(x). Notice the steps shrink on their own as the slope flattens out near the minimum, no separate rule needed for that.</p>

      <h2 id="chain-rule-through-mlp">How the chain rule updates a 2-layer MLP</h2>
      <p>Back to the rent MLP: <code>W1</code> (3&times;2) feeds a ReLU hidden layer, <code>W2</code> (1&times;3) turns that into the predicted rent.</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$h = \text{ReLU}(W_1 x + b_1) \qquad \hat{y} = W_2 h + b_2 \qquad L = (y - \hat{y})^2$$
      </div>

      <p><code>W2</code> sits right next to the loss, one hop away. <code>W1</code> doesn't: to know how nudging <code>W1</code> changes <code>L</code>, you have to travel back through <code>W2</code>, back through the ReLU, back through the multiplication by <code>x</code>. The chain rule is just multiplying the local slope of every hop along that path.</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_2} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial W_2} = \underbrace{-2(y-\hat{y})}_{\partial L/\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \underbrace{h}_{\partial \hat{y}/\partial W_2}$$
      </div>

      <div class="formula">
      $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial h} \cdot \frac{\partial h}{\partial z_1} \cdot \frac{\partial z_1}{\partial W_1} = \underbrace{-2(y-\hat{y})}_{\partial L/\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \underbrace{W_2}_{\partial \hat{y}/\partial h} \cdot \underbrace{\mathbb{1}[z_1 > 0]}_{\partial h/\partial z_1} \cdot \underbrace{x}_{\partial z_1/\partial W_1}$$
      </div>

      <p><code>W1</code> is four hops from the loss, so its gradient is a product of four local derivatives instead of two. That <code>&#120783;[z1 &gt; 0]</code> term is ReLU's derivative: 1 where the hidden unit fired, 0 where it didn't. It's a gate, not just a slope, if a unit is dead for this input, its column of <code>W1</code> gets exactly zero gradient no matter how wrong the prediction is. That's the same dead-unit failure mode from before, seen from the gradient's side instead of the output's.</p>

      </div>

    <section>
      <h2 id="stochastic-gradient-descent">SGD: why batching is required</h2>
      <p>Every gradient so far came from the whole dataset: 4 rentals for the MLP, all of them, every single step. That's <strong>batch gradient descent</strong>, computing the exact gradient by averaging over every training example:</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$\nabla L(w) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \nabla L_i(w)$$
      </div>

      <p>4 rentals is nothing. Real datasets aren't 4 rows, they're millions. Imagine 1,000,000 rentals instead: computing the exact gradient now means touching all 1,000,000 rows, on every single step, thousands of steps in a row. That's the problem, not the math, the sheer cost of it.</p>

      <p><strong>Stochastic gradient descent (SGD)</strong> doesn't compute the exact gradient. It estimates it from a small random sample, a mini-batch, of size <code>B</code>:</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$\nabla L(w) \approx \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i \in \text{batch}} \nabla L_i(w) \qquad B \ll N$$
      </div>

      <p>That estimate is noisy, a random 8 rentals won't have exactly the same average as all 1,000,000, so the direction it points is slightly wrong most steps. But it's cheap: 8 rentals instead of 1,000,000. Cheap enough that you can take far more steps in the same amount of time, and the noise mostly cancels out over many steps.</p>

      <div class="lr-widget">
        <iframe id="sgdBatchFrame" src="/blogs/dl-101/sgd_batch_chalk.html" title="Chalkboard figure: full-batch gradient descent versus mini-batch SGD converging over the same time budget" loading="lazy" style="width:100%;height:460px;border:0;display:block"></iframe>
        <p class="lr-caption">Same clock, different batch size. Full-batch computes 4 exact gradients, 4,000,000 rentals seen in total, and is still short of the target. Mini-batch computes 20 noisy gradients from just 160 rentals total, wobbles along the way, and gets there first.</p>
      </div>

      <p>This is why batching is required at any real scale: not because the exact gradient is wrong, it's the best direction there is, but because computing it exactly is too expensive to do at every step. A noisy estimate you can compute thousands of times over beats an exact answer you can only afford a handful of times. <code>B = 1</code> is the purest form of SGD, one example per step; in practice "SGD" almost always means mini-batches somewhere between a handful and a few hundred examples.</p>

    <section>
      <h2 id="optimizers">Optimizers</h2>
      <p>Every update rule used so far has had the same shape. Whatever computed the gradient, batch or mini-batch, the step itself is:</p>

      <div class="formula">
      $$w \leftarrow w - \eta \, \nabla L(w)$$
      </div>

      <p>An <strong>optimizer</strong> is whatever turns a gradient into that step. The gradient only ever tells you a direction, uphill; the optimizer decides how much of it to actually use. In its plainest form, that decision is just one number: the learning rate, <code>&eta;</code>. Everything you've watched so far, the line fit, the rent MLP, the chain-rule walkthrough, has been this same plain-SGD optimizer underneath.</p>

      <p>Starting at <code>x = 3</code>, each step moves opposite the slope: <code>x &larr; x &minus; learning_rate &times; f'(x)</code>. The steps shrink on their own as the slope flattens out near the minimum, no separate rule needed for that. But that self-shrinking only helps if the learning rate itself is reasonable. Push it too low or too high and the same rule fails in opposite directions:</p>

      <div class="lr-widget">
        <iframe id="lrCompareFrame" src="/blogs/dl-101/lr_compare_chalk.html" title="Chalkboard figure: three learning rates on f(x) = x squared, too low, good, and too high" loading="lazy" style="width:100%;height:460px;border:0;display:block"></iframe>
        <p class="lr-caption">Same curve, same starting point, three learning rates. Too low crawls, still far from the minimum after 5 steps. Good lands right on it. Too high overshoots past the minimum every step, and each overshoot is worse than the last: it diverges instead of converging.</p>
      </div>

      <p>The high-learning-rate panel is worth sitting with: the gradient still shrinks as the point moves away from the minimum on each swing, that part never stops being true, but the step size (<code>learning_rate &times; gradient</code>) is still big enough to fly clean over to the other side, land somewhere worse, and swing back even farther next time. A shrinking gradient doesn't save you from a badly chosen learning rate.</p>

      <p>An optimizer only ever knows the slope right where it's standing. On a loss landscape with more than one dip, that's a real limitation, it can walk downhill in good faith and still end up somewhere that isn't the best place overall:</p>

      <figure class="figure">
        <div class="figure-frame">
          <a href="https://miro.medium.com/1*ZC9qItK9wI0F6BwSVYMQGg.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><img src="https://miro.medium.com/1*ZC9qItK9wI0F6BwSVYMQGg.png" alt="A bumpy loss curve with several shallow local minima and one deeper global minimum, illustrating how gradient descent can settle into a local dip instead of the best overall solution."></a>
        </div>
      </figure>

      <p>A local minimum is a dip that looks like the bottom from wherever the optimizer currently stands, the slope is zero right there, so plain gradient descent has no reason to keep moving. The global minimum is the lowest point on the whole landscape, which might be a completely different dip, one the optimizer never sees because it's only ever looking at the local slope. This is one more place SGD's noise earns its keep: a mini-batch gradient is a slightly wrong direction on purpose, and that wrongness is sometimes exactly enough to knock the point out of a shallow local dip that a perfectly smooth, exact, full-batch gradient would have settled into and stopped.</p>
